## The problem

Support and internal teams at Benevity spent too much time hunting for answers that already existed somewhere — buried in Confluence docs or a Slack thread from three months ago.

The same questions came up again and again. Finding an answer meant either searching across multiple tools, or pinging a colleague who might not respond for hours.

## What we built

A Slack-based AI assistant connected to the internal knowledge base — available to support and CS employees directly in the tool they already lived in.

It answers questions in plain language, grounded in actual company docs, with the source shown so employees can verify before acting on it.

## The product decisions that made it trustworthy

Building the assistant was the easy part. Making it trustworthy was where the real product work happened.

**Source scoping.** I decided what to index and what to leave out — Confluence pages, Slack threads, and Google Workspace docs, filtered for what was current and maintained. Not every document. A chatbot that confidently answers from a doc that's two years out of date is worse than no chatbot at all.

**Access rules.** I defined two tiers — standard employees and power users — and made sure permissions were enforced at retrieval time, not just in the UI. The system checks what a user can see before fetching content, not after.

**Cite everything.** Every answer required a source citation. Employees could click through and verify rather than just trust the output. This was the single decision that drove adoption — people used it because they could check it.

## Outcome

**Support employees spent 30% less time on tickets on average.** The main win wasn't deflection — it was speed. Instead of searching Confluence or pinging a colleague, they got the answer in seconds, with a link to the source.

The LLM underneath started as OpenAI GPT and later migrated under a different project owner, but the decisions about sources, access, and citations stayed the same throughout.

> The hard part of an AI feature isn't the model — it's making it trustworthy. I decided what it could see, when to show it, and who could access what. That belief now carries directly into every AI build I do.
