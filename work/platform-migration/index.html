The situation

Benevity was sunsetting a legacy platform and migrating clients to its successor. Migrations like this are quietly dangerous — long-standing accounts build on configurations nobody fully documents, and a bad transition triggers churn.

20 accounts were exposed. Enterprise and mid-market clients whose setups had grown organically over years. Someone had to find out what was actually there before moving anything.

Discovering the gaps

The two platforms didn't have full feature parity. I exported raw data from both databases and compared them field by field — looking for mismatches, missing fields, and anything that would break on import. Then I walked the actual user flows on both platforms side by side to document what each could and couldn't do.

Not every gap was equal. Some were minor. Others — like EU data hosting — were hard blockers regardless of feature parity. And some were worth fixing: features that high-revenue, high-engagement clients depended on that the new platform simply didn't have yet. Those became integration priorities.

Deciding what to build

For the gaps worth closing, I worked with the team to prioritise which features to integrate into the new platform before migration. The criteria were straightforward: accounts with the highest ARR and most active usage got priority. It wasn't about building everything — it was about protecting the accounts where a missing feature would cause churn.

The Go/No-Go assessment

For each of the 20 accounts, I built a risk score: what features did they depend on, were the gaps resolved, and were there any data residency concerns. Every account landed in one of three buckets.

Ready to migrate
  • Feature parity confirmed
  • No data residency concerns
  • Configurations mapped
Feature gap — integration planned
  • Missing features identified
  • Prioritised by revenue & engagement
  • Features integrated before migration
GDPR blocker — migration paused
  • EU accounts on US-hosted infrastructure
  • Hard data residency requirement
  • Escalated before any migration attempt

The GDPR cases were the most critical find. Several European accounts had strict data residency requirements — and the new platform's infrastructure was US-hosted. Identifying that early meant we could have the right conversation before it became a cancellation.

Historical data migration

Beyond the feature gaps, I also mapped data fields between the two systems to prepare for migrating historical records — making sure nothing was lost or mismatched in the transfer. This was the less visible part of the work but essential to a clean handover.

How it landed

The CSMs used the risk assessments to lead the client conversations — presenting it as "here's what this means for your setup" rather than "we're moving you." That framing mattered.

~$1.8M ARR retained across the 20 accounts. Migration risks caught before they became cancellations. And the structured Go/No-Go process gave the CS team a framework they could reuse across the full migration — not just the 20 accounts in scope.

Good migration work is mostly discovery and judgment. Find what's hidden, assess honestly, and make the call with the revenue and the user in mind — not just the technical checklist.
